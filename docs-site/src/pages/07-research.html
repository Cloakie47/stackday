Research: the weekly report and the daily note

Every Monday, Stackday publishes a research report on the six assets you can also borrow against. Code gathers and computes every number with its source; AI models only interpret, and a person approves the report before anyone sees it.

Once a week you get a plain-language briefing on Bitcoin, Ether, gold, NVIDIA, Apple and the S&P 500: what happened last week, what the charts and the economy look like, what could happen next, and a general view such as "hold" or "avoid new buys".

What is in the weekly report

For each of the six assets, its own page on Research with these parts:

  1. Last week: the price move and what drove it, with sources.
  2. Chart read on daily, weekly and monthly bars: trend, support and resistance, patterns found by code, and how volatile the asset is against its own history. Each pattern has a confidence level and the price that would cancel it.
  3. Market conditions: rates and the Fed, Treasury yields, the dollar, and asset-specific signals (funding rates and stablecoin supply for crypto, real yields for gold).
  4. Geopolitics and news: dated headlines from reputable outlets and official sources. Company press releases count as one side, never the only source.
  5. Week ahead: Fed meetings, inflation and jobs releases, options expiry.
  6. Scenarios: bull, base and bear cases, with rough probabilities that add up to 100%, the price levels involved and what would trigger each.
  7. This week's view: Accumulate, Hold, Reduce or Avoid new buys, with a conviction level (low, medium or high), a buy zone if any, the price that would cancel the view, and the two or three reasons that matter most.
  8. Borrowing note: what a large drop would mean for someone borrowing against this asset, for example how a 20% fall moves a loan at half its limit, with a reminder that the emergency rule exists.
  9. All facts used and the asset's scorecard.

Then one page for the whole report: this week's ideas (a short ranked list across the six, written as general example splits for a cautious and a growth-minded reader, never orders for a person) and last week's scorecard.

How a report is made

The structure follows the published TradingAgents research design (analyst roles, then a bull and bear debate, then a risk review). Stackday took the idea only and wrote its own code. The daily note uses a short version: one triage pass on a cheaper model, a stronger model for big movers or answers that fail checks, then the risk check.

How scores work

  • Direction: each daily note is checked after 1 and 5 trading days. Did the price move the way the view said?
  • Levels: did support and resistance hold or break as described?
  • Calibration: over weeks, do high-conviction calls come true more often than low-conviction ones? Shown per conviction level.
  • Facts: the share of claims with a valid source. The target is 100%.
  • Usefulness: the reviewers grade each note from 1 to 5.

The weekly scorecard marks every view from last week as Right, Wrong, Mixed or No data. Wrong calls stay visible. The report becomes a paid product only after four Mondays in a row with every fact sourced, no corrections, an average grade of 4 or more and calibration that is not clearly off, and after a legal check. Until then it is a free preview and the paywall is off.

When a source fails or has no key, the item is a "no reading" line and nothing is filled in. ETF flows, Fed futures and central bank gold buying have no source yet and always show "no reading". The S&P 500 index level from FRED is never published (its licence forbids it); the token's own price is shown instead.

  • Plans: the planned pricing (one Monday report or a month, paid in test USDC). The paywall is off.

Edge cases and limits

  • Sample data. When there is no approved live report (for example the engine is switched off), the pages show a hand-made sample with a clear "Sample data" banner.
  • Short history. The on-chain stock price feeds start in August 2026, so there is no 200-day average or 52-week range yet. Those items say "no reading".
  • Cost caps. A run stops if it would spend more than $2 on models (the weekly run $4, $6 a day in total), and a failed run writes nothing rather than a partial report.
  • No edge claimed. The team found no credible evidence that a weekly news signal beats the market after costs. The report explains and flags risk; it does not promise returns.
  • Not advice. Every page carries the disclaimer: research only, not financial advice, past calls can be wrong, and tokenized assets can lose value.
